Legal & Banking Foundation

The Issue

Most first-time founders waste their entire first day on entity formation, EIN application, and bank-account opening. The EIN alone can take an hour through the IRS website on a bad day, and bank account opening usually requires an in-person or video-verification step. We start Hour 1 with a real entity, a real bank account, and a real card — so the 8-hour clock is spent on the things that actually move the business: finding the product, building the store, running ads.

Hour One

Idea Generation & Idea Research

The Issue

Many first-time founders start with a demographic ("women 25–40") or a vague vibe ("eco-friendly stuff"). This can be additive at the end of the day, but that's too broad to act on.

Hour 1 should focus on generating a shortlist of specific ideas, how:

  • Unique Viewpoint / Advantage: where do you have a unique viewpoint, experience, or competitive advantage from a hobby, job, or community
  • Problem Solving: where do you see a problem (look for consumer or business complaints or personal concerns)
  • Combinations: what things are not combined today that would be compelling when combined
  • Aiding Others: what could you offer that will help someone already committing substantial capex and/or growing significantly. Helping others achieve their goals is most likely a winning solution for you.
  • Moving ideas across geographies and sectors: what is working someplace now and has not been developed in another area (geography or sector).

Note: There are very few truly "new" ideas — don't expect or try to create something no one has ever thought of and where there are no competitors. The real goal is to understand the competition and how you can differentiate, avoid, or co-exist.

There is no way to avoid the fact that more risk and more work create higher margins — understand the model that can be achieved based on these factors.

We will choose a handful of ideas to take into Hour 02 (Validate Demand & Refine / Pick Hero Products): 2 to 5 ideas moving into Hour 02.

Sources to aid in this selection process are below.

Hour Two

Validate Demand & Refine / Pick Hero Products

The Issue

Research each idea with hard data — search volume, sales velocity, trend curves, and what actual customers are complaining about.

Part of the idea consideration process is understanding the distribution / fulfillment model:

  • Website sales with print-on-demand (lowest risk, thin margins)
  • Website sales with drop-shipping (low risk, slow shipping, low margins)
  • Wholesale by website or sales reps (mid risk, better margins)
  • Custom products distributed directly or through distributors (highest effort, best margins)

Ascertain which is the best idea by using the research and trend tools below; some tools in Hour 1 may still be applicable — the toolset builds each hour. Time to decide on one or two ideas.

CoWork Angle · Hour 2

Once we pick a product or service, ongoing listening and refinement becomes a recurring agentic workflow. Hour Three

Sourcing

The Issue

Where your product comes from determines your margin, shipping speed, quality control, and how much capital you need upfront. Print-on-demand and dropshipping require zero inventory cash but give the worst margins and slowest shipping. Wholesale and custom manufacturing need real money upfront but unlock the margins that make a real business. For a service, "sourcing" means recruiting providers (marketplace), seeding members (network), or sourcing data/content (info app).

Hour Four

Brand, Messaging & Store Build

The Issue

Founders waste hours on logos and color palettes. Your brand at launch needs four things: a name people can spell, a domain you own, a one-line value proposition, and visuals that look intentional. You will redesign everything within six months once you know who actually buys. Don't optimize before you have data. Service businesses don't need Shopify — info apps need creator platforms, marketplaces need Webflow + Sharetribe or no-code, communities need Circle/Mighty.

Hour Five

Product Pages & Payments

The Issue

This is where most stores leak money. Bad photos, feature-led copy ("100% cotton, 200gsm") instead of benefit-led ("the t-shirt that doesn't pill after three washes"), broken checkout, no clear shipping or returns info. Customers bounce in seconds if any of this feels off. Getting basics right on photos, copy, and a working checkout puts you ahead of most launches. For a service, "product page" is your offer page and "checkout" is recurring billing — Stripe Billing, Paddle, Lemon Squeezy, or Memberstack handle it.

Hour Six

Operations, Inventory & Fulfillment

The Issue

Most first-timers underestimate this section. "Operations" means everything between a customer clicking "buy" and them opening the box. Pack orders yourself (cheap, slow, doesn't scale), or outsource to a 3PL (third-party logistics — they hold inventory, pick, pack, ship). Most 3PLs have minimums that don't make sense until 50–100+ orders/month, so plan to self-fulfill at first and migrate when packing eats more than 5 hrs/week. For a service, "operations" is provider onboarding, quality control, dispute handling, payouts — and the four of us coordinate, never deliver.

CoWork Angle · Hour 7

Once Stripe, Shopify, and the bank/credit card are connected, schedule Claude CoWork to run a weekly money pass: pull Shopify's revenue/refund/fees report, pull bank transaction export and credit card statement, reconcile against QuickBooks (or the Excel ledger), flag anything that looks like a duplicate charge or a subscription we forgot, and email the team a Monday-morning cash position with current runway in weeks. That single recurring workflow removes the most common reason small ecom businesses fail in year one — losing track of where the money actually went. Setup cost: ~30 minutes once. Ongoing human time: ~10 minutes per week to review the email.

Hour Eight

Launch, Marketing & Content Engine

The Issue

Most work happens after launch, but you need infrastructure set up before the first dollar of ad spend. Key distinction: organic content (free posts on your own social, algorithm-distributed) vs. paid ads (you pay Meta, TikTok, Google to put creative in front of specific people). Different goals, different creative — content built for organic usually underperforms as paid ads, and vice versa. Most ecom brands run both: paid drives sales now, organic builds an audience you can retarget. Service marketing leans heavier on Google Search, SEO, LinkedIn, and case studies than scroll-stopping creative.

Critical · Set Up Before Any Traffic

Set up Klaviyo and Google Analytics 4 before you drive any traffic. Retroactively building either is painful — you can't recover events that weren't tracked. Klaviyo is free up to 250 contacts; GA4 is free at any volume. Both go live before your first ad dollar or first organic post.

Critical · The AI Content Saturation Trap

AI content tools have a saturation problem. The brands winning aren't the ones posting the most AI content — they're the ones posting AI content with a real human point of view layered on top. Use these tools to remove busywork, not to replace having something to say. A perfectly produced AI carousel with no insight will lose to a phone-shot video with one strong opinion.
