# The 8-Hour Launch Master Plan

*For a product business — or a service business we orchestrate rather than deliver ourselves.*

A working reference for launching an online product business — **or a service that we can offer.** If it is a service, it has to be one of: **connecting demand to supply** (a marketplace or matching service), **a networking service** (community, directory, lead-gen), or **an app that provides information** (data, insights, education, software-as-a-service).

Each section below explains the issues you'll face and the companies that solve them. Where the answer differs for a service vs. a product, both paths are called out.

---

## Hour 1: Idea Generation & Idea Research

**The issue:** Most first-time founders start with a demographic ("women 25–40") or a vague vibe ("eco-friendly stuff"). That's too broad to act on. Hour 1 is two jobs: **generate** a shortlist of specific ideas (ideally where we have unfair advantage from a hobby, job, or community), then **research** each one with hard data — search volume, sales velocity, trend curves, and what actual customers are complaining about. Consider the demographic, community, environment, and the ability to access customers. Consider the fulfillment / distribution model: print-on-demand (lowest risk, thin margins), dropshipping (low risk, no inventory, slow shipping), wholesale buying (mid risk, better margins), or your own custom products (highest effort, best margins and brand).

**If it's a service, the model question changes.** Instead of fulfillment, ask: are we a *marketplace* (connecting buyers and sellers, taking a cut — e.g. a niche Thumbtack), a *networking/community* play (paid membership, directory, B2B intros), or an *information app* (subscription content, data feed, lightweight SaaS)? All three can hit $1M with no physical inventory, but they have very different unit economics and acquisition curves. Marketplaces are hardest (chicken-and-egg supply/demand), info apps are easiest to build but hardest to differentiate, networking sits in between.

**Where to go for solutions:**

*Idea generation sources (curated databases, AI generators, the unfair-advantage prompt):*
- **[IdeaBrowser](https://www.ideabrowser.com/)** — AI-curated business ideas with market data and opportunity scoring. Best single site in this category.
- **[Trends.vc](https://trends.vc/)** — long-form trend reports; each one is essentially a researched business idea
- **[Starter Story](https://www.starterstory.com/ideas)** — case studies of real founders hitting revenue milestones, by category
- **[Failory](https://www.failory.com/)** — failed and successful startup teardowns; see what's already been tried
- **[Validator AI](https://www.validatorai.com/)** — give it a one-line idea, it returns a critique and market check
- **Claude / ChatGPT / Gemini** — underrated. Prompt: *"20 niche ecom ideas where buyer is a hobbyist with disposable income, market $50–$500M, products POD-able"*
- **The unfair-advantage prompt** — no tool, a method. Sit the four founders down for 30 minutes: what does each of us know more about than 95% of people? What problems have we personally paid to solve in the last year? What do our friends/colleagues complain about that we have insight into? The intersection beats anything an idea database surfaces, because you have built-in distribution and credibility from day one.

*Entrepreneurial ideas media (where founders hang out, podcasts and newsletters):*

**CoWork angle for Hour 1:** This is the section where ongoing listening becomes a recurring agentic workflow, not a one-time task. Once we pick a niche, schedule Claude CoWork to run daily or weekly: (a) pull top posts from 5–10 target subreddits matching pain-point keywords, (b) pull X/Reddit posts mentioning competitors, (c) drop a summarized digest into a Word doc, Notion page, or email. The output is a steady stream of customer language for ad copy, content ideas, and product gaps — and it costs us no human hours after setup. F5Bot and Syften both have webhooks/email outputs that plug into this pattern cleanly.

## Hour 2: Validate Demand + Pick Hero Products

**The issue:** "Before you build anything, confirm people are already spending money in your category. Pick one *hero product* (the thing you're known for) and 1–2 complementary items. Launching with 50 SKUs splits your attention and budget; launching with one lets you test, learn, and double down.

**Service equivalent:** Pick one *hero offer* — a single, sharply-defined service tier or info product — not a menu. Validate by checking whether people are already paying for adjacent solutions (Reddit threads asking for it, competitor pricing pages, paid Facebook groups, existing Substacks/courses, freelancer rates on Upwork or Fiverr). For marketplaces, validate the *supply* side first — if you can't get 20 providers committed to list, demand doesn't matter.

**Pair this with the social listening tools from Hour 1.** Run a [GummySearch](https://gummysearch.com/) query for "I wish there was," "is there a tool that," or "recommend" across the subreddits where your customers live — those threads are the demand validation. If 200 people are asking for something adjacent to your hero product and there's no clean answer in the comments, that's a green light. If the top reply is "just use [existing tool]," that's a red one.

**Where to go for solutions:**
- **[Amazon Best Sellers](https://www.amazon.com/Best-Sellers/zgbs)** — free, real-time view of what's actually selling
- **[Etsy search](https://www.etsy.com/)** — best for handmade, gift, or design-driven categories
- **[TikTok Creative Center](https://ads.tiktok.com/business/creativecenter/)** — see which products are going viral on TikTok right now
- **[Google Trends](https://trends.google.com/)** — confirm the category isn't seasonal or declining
- **[Keepa](https://keepa.com/)** — Amazon price and sales-rank history (catches fake "trending" products)

## Hour 3: Sourcing

**The issue:** Where your product comes from determines your margin, shipping speed, quality control, and how much capital you need upfront. Print-on-demand and dropshipping require zero inventory cash but give you the worst margins and slowest shipping. Wholesale and custom manufacturing need real money upfront but unlock the margins that make a real business.

**If it's a service, "sourcing" means sourcing supply, not goods.** For a marketplace, this is recruiting providers (contractors, experts, sellers, creators). For a networking service, it's seeding the initial member base or directory. For an info app, it's sourcing the content or data — your own expertise, licensed data feeds, scraped public data, AI-generated content with human review, or contributor/creator networks. Plan to spend Hour 3 on **outreach lists and sample agreements** rather than supplier samples. Tools shift accordingly: Apollo / Clay for prospecting, Calendly for intro calls, DocuSign or PandaDoc for provider terms.

**If the idea is an app, marketplace, or info-app/SaaS,** "sourcing" means finding people or tools to build it. For an 8-hour launch, the only realistic options are no-code (Bubble, Glide, Softr, Sharetribe, Webflow + Memberstack) or AI-assisted "vibe coding" (Lovable, Replit Agent, v0, Bolt.new, Cursor, Claude Code). For longer-horizon paths — hiring freelancers, fractional CTOs, or buying existing software — see [If You Had Longer to Develop an App](app_build_paths.html).

**Where to go for solutions:**

*Print-on-demand (zero inventory, supplier ships when you sell):*
- **[Printify](https://printify.com/)** — cheapest, widest product catalog, multiple print partners
- **[Printful](https://www.printful.com/)** — better quality control, slightly higher prices
- **[Gelato](https://www.gelato.com/)** — best for international shipping (local print partners worldwide)

*Dropshipping (supplier ships, you never touch product):*
- **[Spocket](https://www.spocket.co/)** — US/EU suppliers, faster shipping than AliExpress
- **[AutoDS](https://www.autods.com/)** — automation-heavy, good for scaling
- **[Zendrop](https://www.zendrop.com/)** — Shopify-focused, decent supplier vetting
- **[DSers](https://www.dsers.com/)** — official AliExpress partner, cheapest but slowest shipping

*Wholesale (you buy inventory, hold it, sell it):*
- **[Faire](https://www.faire.com/)** — wholesale marketplace with net-60 terms for new retailers
- **[Abound](https://helloabound.com/)** — similar to Faire, growing brand selection
- **[Tundra](https://www.tundra.com/)** — no fees on either side
- **[Wonnda](https://wonnda.com/)** — European wholesale + private-label hybrid (cosmetics, supplements, food, household goods). Faire-meets-Alibaba.

*Custom manufacturing (your own designed products):*
- **[Alibaba](https://www.alibaba.com/)** — global manufacturers, requires diligence on samples and contracts
- **[Pietra](https://pietrastudio.com/)** — curated suppliers for beauty, jewelry, apparel
- **[Maker's Row](https://makersrow.com/)** — US-based manufacturers (slower, pricier, but local)

## Hour 4: Brand + Messaging + Store Build

**The issue:** Founders waste hours on logos and color palettes. Your brand at launch needs four things: a name people can spell, a domain you own, a one-line value proposition, and visuals that look intentional. That's it. You will redesign everything within six months once you know who actually buys from you. Don't optimize before you have data.

**Service businesses don't need Shopify; they need the right front door.** Information app or content subscription → use a creator platform (Substack, Beehiiv, Ghost, Memberstack, MemberSpace, or Circle). Marketplace → typically needs Webflow + a marketplace builder like Sharetribe, or a no-code stack like Bubble or Softr on top of Airtable. Networking/community → Circle, Mighty Networks, or Geneva. Lead-gen / "service productized" (one-page site that books calls) → Webflow, Framer, or Carrd. The brand basics (name, domain, one-liner, visuals) are identical across product and service.

**Where to go for solutions:**

*Domain registration:*

- **[Namecheap](https://www.namecheap.com/)** — cheap, decent UI
- **[Porkbun](https://porkbun.com/)** — cheap, clean interface, good for niche TLDs

*Logo and brand identity:*
- **[Looka](https://looka.com/)** — AI-generated logo and brand kit, fast
- **[Canva](https://www.canva.com/)** — DIY logo and brand assets, free tier works
- **[Brandmark](https://brandmark.io/)** — AI logo generator, good typography

*Store platform:*
- **[Shopify](https://www.shopify.com/)** — default for ecom, fastest setup, every integration. *See [Additional Build Comments](build_paths.html) for how Claude can help build the storefront — Choice #1 is starting from a free Shopify theme (Dawn, Sense, Craft) and customizing it.*
- **[Squarespace Commerce](https://www.squarespace.com/ecommerce-website)** — prettier templates, less flexibility

- **[Etsy](https://www.etsy.com/sell)** — built-in traffic for handmade/vintage, but you don't own the customer

## Hour 5: Product Pages + Payments

**The issue:** This is where most stores leak money. Bad photos, feature-led copy ("100% cotton, 200gsm") instead of benefit-led ("the t-shirt that doesn't pill after three washes"), broken checkout, no clear shipping or returns info. Customers bounce in seconds if any of this feels off. The good news: getting the basics right on photos, copy, and a working checkout puts you ahead of most launches.

**For a service, "product page" is your offer page or pricing page, and "checkout" is recurring billing.** No photos to worry about — but every word matters more, because a service is sold on outcomes and proof, not specs. You need: a crisp pain-point headline, a clear "what you get" list, social proof (even one testimonial counts at launch), pricing tiers, and a frictionless way to start (book a call, start a trial, sign up). Payments shift from one-time to subscription / per-transaction — Stripe Billing, Stripe Connect (for marketplaces taking a cut), Paddle, Lemon Squeezy, or Memberstack handle this. Skip Shopify Payments entirely if there are no physical goods.

**Where to go for solutions:**

*Payment processing:*
- **[Shopify Payments](https://www.shopify.com/payments)** — built into Shopify, lowest fees if you use Shopify
- **[Stripe](https://stripe.com/)** — works everywhere, developer-friendly
- **[PayPal](https://www.paypal.com/us/business)** — adds buyer trust, some customers won't check out without it
- **[Shop Pay](https://shop.app/shop-pay)** — Shopify's express checkout, measurably higher conversion

*Product photography:*
- **[Canva](https://www.canva.com/)** — quick mockups, lifestyle composites
- **[Pebblely](https://pebblely.com/)** — AI-generated product photos against any background
- **[Claid.ai](https://claid.ai/)** — AI photo enhancement and background generation

## Hour 6: Operations, Inventory, Fulfillment

**The issue:** This is the section most first-timers underestimate. "Operations" means everything between a customer clicking "buy" and them opening the box. You'll need to decide: do you pack orders yourself from your spare room (cheap, slow, doesn't scale), or outsource to a 3PL (third-party logistics — they hold your inventory, pick, pack, and ship for you)? Most 3PLs have minimums that don't make sense until you're doing 50–100+ orders a month, so plan to self-fulfill at first and migrate when packing eats more than 5 hours a week of your time. You'll also need inventory management software the moment you have more than ~20 SKUs or sell on more than one channel.

**For a service, "operations" is fulfillment of the service itself — and since none of us has time to do anything physical, this is the most important section to get right.** The whole business has to be designed so the four of us are *coordinating and orchestrating*, not delivering. That means: the work is done by providers (marketplace), automation/AI (info app), or member contributions (network). Operations becomes provider onboarding, quality control, dispute handling, payouts, and SLA monitoring. Useful stack: **Zapier or n8n** for workflow automation, **Airtable or Notion** as the backend, **Stripe Connect** for paying providers, **Calendly + Zoom** for any human-touch points, **Intercom or Crisp** for triage. The 3PL/inventory tools below don't apply.

**Where to go for solutions:**

- **[Saltbox](https://saltbox.com/)** — hybrid 3PL + workspace; warehouses with co-working built in. Good for makers shipping their own product.

*Amazon-integrated fulfillment:*

## Hour 7: Legal, Finance, Customer Operations

**The issue:** Running revenue through a personal Venmo or checking account is a tax nightmare and creates personal liability. You need an LLC, an EIN, a business bank account, basic terms and privacy pages, and a way to handle sales tax (which got vastly more complex after the 2018 Wayfair ruling — you may owe sales tax in states where you have no physical presence). You also need a customer support system before your first complaint hits, not after.

**If it's a service:** Different legal surface. Sales tax matters less (most pure services and digital info products are not taxable in most states, but **SaaS and digital goods are taxable in a growing list — check state-by-state**). What matters more: a real Terms of Service, a Privacy Policy that covers data you collect, and — for marketplaces — provider/contractor agreements (W-9s, 1099 thresholds), refund and dispute policies, and clear language disclaiming liability for what providers do. Info apps may also need disclaimers ("not legal/financial/medical advice"). Templates from [Termly](https://termly.io/), [iubenda](https://www.iubenda.com/), or [Bonsai](https://www.hellobonsai.com/) cover the basics; for marketplace terms, talk to a lawyer once we cross $10K MRR.

## Hour 8: Launch, Marketing, Content Engine

**The issue:** This is where most of the work happens *after* launch, but you need the infrastructure set up before your first dollar of ad spend. The biggest distinction to understand: **organic content** (free posts on your own social accounts, where the platform's algorithm decides who sees them) and **paid ads** (you pay Meta, TikTok, or Google to put your creative in front of specific people). They serve different goals and need different creative — content built for organic engagement usually underperforms as paid ads, and vice versa. Most ecom brands run both: paid ads drive sales now, organic content builds long-term brand and an audience you can retarget.

**Service marketing leans heavier on content and intent.** Products win on visual scroll-stopping creative; services win on trust, expertise, and being found at the moment of need. That shifts the channel mix: **Google Ads (Search) and SEO** punch above their weight because people Google "the problem"; **LinkedIn** matters for B2B services and networking plays; **YouTube long-form and a podcast** are common organic engines; **referral and affiliate programs** convert better for services than products; and **Reddit, niche Slacks, and Discords** are often where the first 100 customers come from. Paid social still works (especially TikTok/Meta for info apps and consumer marketplaces) but expect higher CAC and longer payback. Most tools below still apply — Klaviyo, Triple Whale, GA4, Motion — but reviews/UGC tools matter less than case studies and testimonials.

> **⚠ Critical · Set up before any traffic:** Set up **Klaviyo and Google Analytics 4 before you drive any traffic**. Retroactively building either is painful — you can't recover events that weren't tracked. Klaviyo is free up to 250 contacts; GA4 is free at any volume. Both go live before your first ad dollar or first organic post.

> **⚠ Critical · The AI content saturation trap:** AI content tools have a saturation problem. The brands winning **aren't the ones posting the most AI content** — they're the ones posting AI content with a real human point of view layered on top. Use these tools to remove busywork, not to replace having something to say. A perfectly produced AI carousel with no insight will lose to a phone-shot video with one strong opinion.

*Paid acquisition planning — see [ROAS Calculator & Cost Matrix](roas_calculator.html) for an interactive break-even ROAS / max-CPA / budget-forecast tool, a 2026 cost matrix across the seven channels we'd actually consider, and a starting allocation for our first $5K/month of spend.* Key points the calculator enforces: **break-even ROAS = 1 ÷ gross margin** (at 55% margin you need 1.82x just to not lose money); **target ROAS = break-even + 25% profit buffer**; **max CPA = AOV × margin**. The 2026 platform medians built into the matrix: Google Search 4.5x, Google Shopping 5.0x, Meta 2.5–3.0x, TikTok 1.4–2.0x, Pinterest 2.0–3.5x, Reddit 2.3–4.7x, Email 36x+ (owned). The prevailing 2026 default for ecom is the **70/30 split**: 70% to Google to capture existing demand, 30% to Meta/TikTok to create new demand. Adjust toward Meta/TikTok for visual impulse products; toward Google for things people actively search for.
